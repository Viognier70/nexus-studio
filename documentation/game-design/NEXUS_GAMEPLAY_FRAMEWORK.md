**Order of authority.** Where any future document conflicts with this framework, this framework prevails, except against the parent directive and the Constitution. **How to read this document.** It is not a systems catalogue. It is an argument about why Nexus is fun. The chapters answer, in different lights, one question:

> **What does the player actually do that is enjoyable for the next 100 hours?**

If any passage below stops answering that question, the passage is wrong.

## 1. The promise

Nexus is a game about the pleasure of running a living place until you *know* it.

It is not a course. It is not a business school. It is not a simulator you graduate from. It is a game — with the emergent life of *The Sims*, the story-generation of *RimWorld*, the widening horizons of *Civilization*, the director's perspective of *Command & Conquer*, and the indirect authorship of *Cities: Skylines* — set in a real Swedish village, played from the chair of the proprietor.

The player is never a hero. Never levels up. Never collects. Never fights.

The player *builds a place, watches it come alive, and grows into someone who can read it well.* The joy that carries the 100th hour is the same joy that carried the 1st, and it deepens with every hour in between: the joy of paying attention to a world that answers back.

### 1.1 Three snapshots

**Hour 1.** A gray-lit June evening in Grythyttan. You have just opened. Two staff. Four guests seated. A couple hesitates over the wine list at table three. The wine list is one you chose. The staffing is a hire you made. The hesitation is a fact of the world. You zoom in a little. You cannot yet read what the hesitation means.

**Hour 10.** You now know what that hesitation means, at mid-week, at this hour, with a couple of about that age. Your sommelier knows too — she stands a step closer to table three than she did a week ago. You did not tell her to. You built a culture in which she noticed. You never resolve the hesitation for her; you never will. But the room reads to you now, and you read the room.

**Hour 100.** A woman you have never seated walks in with her father and asks for the corner table. Your host recognises the name because you welcomed her sister to a private dinner two seasons ago. The corner table is set within a minute by staff you trained years ago. You did not lift a finger. You are the director of a place that runs itself under conditions you have shaped for a hundred hours. Somewhere else in Grythyttan another director is watching their own room. When you meet them at a banquet next week you will understand each other without needing to explain.

This is the arc. Everything in this framework serves it.

## 2. What Nexus stands on

Nexus is the union of five kinds of pleasure that other games have earned. Each contributes something specific; each is honoured and each is changed by the union.

**From *The Sims*: emergent behaviour.** In *The Sims*, you set the conditions — a house, a career, a fridge with food in it — and lives happen. You did not script the fight, the affair, the dinner that went well. You made the conditions in which those things could happen. Nexus takes this literally at the scale of a hospitality business. You do not script your staff or your guests. You set the conditions. Behaviour emerges.

**From *RimWorld*: stories out of systems.** In *RimWorld*, colonists have identities, relationships and traits, and events cascade until you have stories you tell your friends. Nexus wants its players to have stories about specific nights, specific staff, specific guests, specific mistakes. Not achievements. Stories. The game exists to produce them.

**From *Civilization*: knowledge changes possibilities.** In *Civ*, a technology researched opens hexes you couldn't see, units you couldn't build, trades you couldn't make. Knowledge in *Civ* is a spine — the game's horizon widens as you learn. Nexus takes this literally and makes it primary (Chapter 5): the more the director knows, the more the director can *see, propose, recognise and attempt*. What is invisible to a novice director is a live option for an experienced one.

**From *Command & Conquer*: the director's perspective.** In *C&C*, you never step into the tank. You allocate, you choose research, you command from above, and when a battle unfolds well you watch your plan work. Nexus takes this posture and refuses to give it up: the player is a director, always, in strategic play (Chapter 3).

**From *Cities: Skylines*: indirect control through systems.** In *Cities*, you zone, road and policy — and citizens do their own thing. Traffic emerges. Neighbourhoods take on character. Problems arise from your choices interacting. The pleasure is diagnostic. Nexus takes this at the scale of a business inside a village: policies and conditions shape outcomes, and the outcomes are legible in the room.

**What Nexus does differently.** Those games are set in fictional or procedural worlds. Nexus is set in real Grythyttan (Chapter 11). Those games treat knowledge as a tech tree or a levelling curve. Nexus treats knowledge as **capital** — the primary form (Chapter 5). Those games reward optimisation, growth, or conquest. Nexus rewards **becoming a good proprietor of a specific place** — a smaller, warmer, and slower ambition, and one no other game has fully attempted.

## 3. The director principle — the felt posture

Before any system, one felt posture: **you are the director**.

Not the chef. Not the server. Not the sommelier. Not the guest. Not the hero. Not the avatar. The director — the person responsible for the *conditions* under which a living place operates.

### 3.1 What this feels like

You feel it every time you watch a service play out and *do not touch* a single person on the floor. You hired them, you briefed them, you set the concept — and now they perform work you cannot perform for them. When they perform it well, the pleasure is that of a conductor whose orchestra is playing well, not of a player who moved a piece. When they perform it badly, the responsibility is yours — but the fix is upstream, in hiring, training, briefing, culture, concept — not in a next click.

### 3.2 Why the director's chair is where the game lives

Every other seat has been done. The chef-simulator, the server-simulator, the tycoon-clicker, the RTS puppet show. None of those games can produce the specific pleasure Nexus is built for: **the pleasure of a place you shaped running well on its own**. That pleasure requires the director's chair and it requires nothing else.

### 3.3 The director's chair is not empty

Sitting in the chair is not passive. It carries the six kinds of decision (Chapter 7), the reading of the room (Chapter 6), the placement of bets, the response to the unexpected, and — above all — the accumulation of knowledge (Chapter 5) that changes what the director can *do next*.

### 3.4 What the director never does

Never puppeteers a staff member across a room. Never selects a guest as a unit. Never controls a body in first person during strategic play. Never opens a spreadsheet that replaces the world. Never watches a numeric HUD dominate the interface. Never levels up. Never receives an unlock chime.

If any of these ever appear in Nexus, the director principle has failed, and the offending mechanic must be removed.

## 4. The seven-beat loop — the shape of every good minute

Every good minute of Nexus, from the tenth minute to the hundredth hour, has the same shape:

**Observation → Decision → Action → Consequence → Reflection → Learning → New opportunities**

This is not a tutorial ladder. It is what a minute of running a place *feels like*. It is the shape of a story, told over and over, in different weights and at different scales.

### 4.1 Observation — the game of paying attention

You read the world at whatever distance you are watching from. The village at village scale. The district at district scale. The room at business scale. The cues are legible without being labelled — a bottle held two seconds too long, a hesitation at the door, a runner circling. The pleasure is the pleasure of noticing. It compounds: a player who has watched for ten hours sees things a player who has watched for one hour cannot. Observation *is* craft.

### 4.2 Decision — the pleasure of choosing between imperfect options

There is no dominant answer in Nexus. A cheaper wine list wins the margin and costs identity. A brisker seating pace serves more covers and shortens the meal. A generous return policy earns social capital and softens economic capital. You are not choosing between right and wrong. You are choosing *what kind of place this is*.

### 4.3 Action — the weight of commitment

Decisions and actions are separate on purpose. The moment of commitment matters: a decision revoked before it acts costs nothing; a decision enacted has weight. When you commit, the world *visibly registers*. Staff behaviour shifts. A supplier acknowledges. A policy indicator moves. The world moves because you moved.

### 4.4 Consequence — the world runs

Consequences unfold over sim time. Often across the rest of a shift. Sometimes across seasons. You *see* the consequence before you *read* the summary. A scenario resolves in the room, not in a popup. You can act again while it plays out. Nothing pauses.

### 4.5 Reflection — a moment to see what you did

After the consequence, the sustainability conditions update with direction, cause and expected consequence. A mentor NPC may pass a comment. A peer player may say something across the shift. The daily rhythm closes for the night. Reflection is short, specific and voluntary — the game does not force a post-service screen. You dwell on what happened or move on. This is where you begin to know what you now know.

### 4.6 Learning — what the loop does to you

The player who has been through the group-arrival scenario three times *reads the fourth arrival differently*. The game does not tell them how. They just do. Learning here is not a token you bank. It is the change in the person playing. This is why the 100th hour is not the 1st hour with more content — it is the 1st hour with a director who now sees.

### 4.7 New opportunities — the world you shaped comes back to you

Because the world changed, new situations arise. A supplier extended credit returns with first pick of the harvest. A staff member who thrived asks for a leadership role. A guest brought their friends. The next loop starts richer than the last, without a content pipeline authoring it. The world is not spawning quests. **The world is remembering what you did and answering.**

### 4.8 Why this loop never gets old

Because at every point in it, the world has more to notice than the player has yet seen, and the director has more to bring to noticing than they had last time. This is why the loop repeats naturally, and why it repeats *warmly*: each turn is not the same turn again. Each turn is a turn with the version of you who came out of the last one.

## 5. Knowledge is the primary capital of Nexus

This is the load-bearing chapter. Every other chapter cross-references it.

### 5.1 Knowledge, not experience points

Nexus does not have experience points. It does not have levels. It does not have skill trees. It does not have unlock chimes. The reason is not aesthetic — it is functional. Experience points measure *time spent*. Nexus is not interested in time spent. Nexus is interested in *what the director has come to see*.

Knowledge replaces XP as the currency of progression. Knowledge is:

- **Earned** by causing or witnessing events that produce recognisable patterns.
- **Held** as a repertoire of specific, named understandings — not a bar or a number.
- **Invested**, not spent — you do not lose it when you use it. You commit it into the organisation, and it becomes the organisation's capability.
- **Compounding** — knowledge held changes what future knowledge you can earn, because it changes what you can *see*.
- **Perishable** — knowledge decays when unused, gets contradicted by shifts in the world, and can be lost when the staff who carried it leave.
- **Shareable** — between players, between mentor and newcomer, through published cases.
- **Transformable** into organisational capability — the organisation's Episteme, Techne and Phronesis (Chapter 10).

### 5.2 The Civilization insight, made primary

*Civilization* proved that knowledge changes possibilities: a researched technology unlocks hexes you couldn't see, units you couldn't build, trades you couldn't make. Nexus takes this insight and makes it the game's spine.

**In Nexus, knowledge changes what the director can imagine, design, propose and recognise.**

- A director without knowledge sees a room. A director with knowledge sees *why* the room is what it is.
- A director without knowledge sees a problem. A director with knowledge sees *which* problem it is.
- A director without knowledge proposes a menu. A director with knowledge proposes a menu that *fits this village, this season, this guest population, this staff*.
- A director without knowledge chooses between options. A director with knowledge sees an option that was not on the list.

This last is the crucial one. In Nexus, deeper knowledge does not merely make you *better at existing choices*. Deeper knowledge *widens the set of choices you can see*. This is the *Civilization* dynamic pulled inside the director's head.

### 5.3 The trajectory: knowledge → action → experience → wisdom

Nexus's inner engine is a spiral:

- **Knowledge becomes action.** You commit an understanding into a decision — a hire, a policy, an investment, a response to a scenario, a change in concept.
- **Action becomes experience.** The world runs. Consequences accumulate over sim time — legible in the room, in guests, in staff, in the log.
- **Experience becomes wisdom.** Repeated exposure to related situations makes you the kind of director who no longer needs to think — you *see* what to do. This is Phronesis (Chapter 10.3). It is not a stat. It is a person you have become.
- **Wisdom becomes new knowledge.** Wisdom sees patterns a novice cannot, and so wisdom produces understandings of higher order — understandings about people, about the market, about culture, about yourself as a director.

The spiral has no end. It is why the game is worth 100 hours.

### 5.4 The director's log

The one place the knowledge economy surfaces in the interface is the **director's log**. The log is not a stat sheet. It reads as a working notebook. Each entry is a named recognition of a pattern the director has seen play out, with the events that produced it attached. You can browse, search, re-read, pin.

The log is not a menu. It is a mind. You are not consulting a database; you are re-reading yourself.

### 5.5 How understandings arise (as gameplay, not education)

Understandings arise through play. Not through reading. Not through lectures. Not through quizzes. Specifically:

- **You do a thing and something happens.** Consequence witnessed is the strongest teacher.
- **You resolve a scenario.** Whatever you chose and whatever followed both write to what you now understand.
- **You experiment.** You change a condition to see what will happen. Attention on outcome produces understandings faster than accidental exposure.
- **You survive a failure.** Losses teach more than successes. A collapsed service you recovered from writes an understanding a smooth service cannot.
- **A peer tells you what they have come to know.** Received as provisional; confirmed by your own experience.
- **A mentor names a pattern you had been living without recognising.** Implicit exposure becomes explicit understanding.

Every one of these is play. None of them is a lesson.

### 5.6 Knowledge invested becomes capability

An understanding on its own is only the director's. Invested, it becomes the *organisation's*. The routes of investment:

- Train staff on the understanding.
- Hire specifically for a role the understanding says is missing.
- Codify the understanding as documented policy that persists through turnover.
- Write it into the concept itself.
- Anchor it in culture, where it propagates through every new hire.

Investment has cost — time, money, disruption of the current organisation through change. Investment is not free and is not instant. It is a bet, and its return arrives across seasons.

### 5.7 Knowledge can be lost

Understandings decay when unused, get contradicted when the world moves, and can walk out of the door when staff leave. This is not punishment. This is what makes attention valuable and what makes the director keep coming back to the log.

### 5.8 Knowledge shared makes a scene, not a hoard

In multiplayer, directors share understandings across shifts, meals and mentorships. The village becomes a knowledge ecology, not a leaderboard. A director who publishes a case is a director whose reputation compounds. A director who mentors a newcomer is a director who becomes an elder of the place. Sharing knowledge in Nexus is not altruistic — it is how a director grows into the role of institution.

### 5.9 What knowledge is deliberately not

- Not a number.
- Not a bar.
- Not a tier.
- Not a tree with unlockable nodes.
- Not a currency you can buy with cash.
- Not a scoreboard.
- Not an achievement list.
- Not something that can be maxed out.

If any of these appear, this chapter has failed.

## 6. What the director does — the verbs

The player's fingers do a small vocabulary of things, and the pleasures those things produce are the pleasures the game runs on.

### 6.1 The universal controls (present at every scale)

- **Pan, zoom, orbit** — move through the continuous camera curve of the Camera Bible. The world does not cut. You slide through scales.
- **Select** — describe a building or person without commanding them (Camera Bible §6.1). Curiosity is a first-class gesture.
- **Focus** — glide inward one comfortable step toward something you want to see closer.
- **Outward** — one comfortable step back, at any time.

### 6.2 The director's verbs and the pleasure each produces

- **Watch a service run.** *The Sims* pleasure: your arrangement working. You made this. The people you hired are doing things you cannot do for them. The service holds. You did not touch it.
- **Read a person.** *RimWorld* pleasure: pattern recognition on a specific character. Over hours, a sommelier's posture becomes a language you speak.
- **Read the room.** Diagnostic pleasure: something is off. You see it before it becomes a scenario. You act — or you wait to see if the room recovers on its own.
- **Adjust a condition.** *Cities: Skylines* pleasure: authorship. You changed a policy and the world is now different. You take responsibility for what happens next.
- **Respond to a scenario.** Judgement pleasure: no response is neutral. Whatever you choose becomes part of what your business *is*.
- **Interview a candidate.** Character encounter: not a list. A person is in front of you.
- **Negotiate with a supplier.** Bet placement inside a relationship: the terms you accept will pay dividends or costs across seasons.
- **Meet a peer** (multiplayer). Shared craft: another director sees what you see, or doesn't.
- **Represent the business publicly.** Recognition earned: you are known now, in a place that knew you as no one an hour ago.
- **Invest capital.** *Civilization* pleasure of the tech commit: you place a bet you cannot easily reverse, and seasons later you find out.
- **Re-read the log.** Continuity of self: you were the director who did that. You are the director who now does this.
- **Sit and think.** The game honours doing nothing on purpose. The world continues. Some of the best decisions are made this way.

### 6.3 What the director does not do

Never puppeteer, never step into first person during strategic play, never race a clock imposed by the game, never consume a spreadsheet as primary interface.

## 7. Decisions and the shape of an hour

Every hour of Nexus contains a small number of decisions. Six textures. Each generates a distinct pleasure. A good hour crosses several; a great hour finds them entangled.

- **Strategic** (concept, target guest, staffing model, financial posture). Slow, weighty, rarely reversible. The pleasure of *taking a position and living inside it*.
- **Operational** (tonight's staffing, tomorrow's mise, this month's reservation policy). Frequent, immediate. The pleasure of *steering the day*.
- **Creative** (menu direction, room feel, cultural stance). Rare, weighty, visible to others. The pleasure of *authorship your peers can recognise*.
- **Ethical** (sourcing, fair work, community, transparency, treatment of failure). Unwelcome, long-tailed, no dominant answer. The pleasure — hard-won — of *carrying consequences*.
- **Social** (which supplier to favour, which guest to name, which peer to help). Small individually, huge in aggregate. The pleasure of *belonging somewhere*.
- **Financial** (invest, save, borrow, expand). Numbers matter; they feed the decision without dominating the interface. The pleasure of *bet placement*.

The most interesting hours are the ones where a social relationship enables an ethical commitment which requires a financial move which shifts the strategic posture and cascades into operational adjustments the next day. Nexus lets decision types entangle. It never flattens them into a single number.

## 8. Uncertainty is why the choice matters

Knowledge improves decisions. Knowledge never removes uncertainty (Constitution §8).

The village weather turns. The season shifts. A guest is in a mood you did not cause. A cook comes off a hard week. A supplier is short today. A local festival crowds the district. A road closes. Another player, in another business, changes what the village is doing tonight.

Knowledge **narrows the outcome distribution without collapsing it**. A well-read director more often chooses responses whose distributions favour a smooth resolution. A long-standing supplier relationship narrows the probability of failure and the severity when it happens. Staff investment tightens the range of a difficult service — disaster becomes rare, excellence becomes common — without predetermining the specific outcome tonight.

Uncertainty is enjoyable when the player believes the outcome was *caused*, not *rolled*. Nexus preserves this by:

- authoring every random draw from a seeded source (Directive §7),
- exposing cause through the sustainability readout — direction, cause, consequence,
- making distributions visible in behaviour rather than numbers, so a bad night for a well-trained team reads as a bad night, not an unfair roll.

Randomness in Nexus is texture. It is never the reason the director wins or loses. The reason is what the director chose, what the world was, and how those met.

## 9. How stories arise in Nexus

Nexus is a story generator.

Not in the *cinematic* sense. In the *RimWorld* sense: stories arise because a system of characters and conditions produces situations you did not script but recognise as meaningful, and *you keep them*. You will remember specific evenings in Nexus the way *RimWorld* players remember specific colonists.

### 9.1 The ingredients of a Nexus story

- **A cast with identity.** Staff have names, histories, temperaments. Regular guests accumulate identity across visits. Suppliers, neighbours, mentors, peers — all specific, all remembered.
- **A place with rhythm.** Grythyttan has weather, seasons, calendars, hours. The village *is doing something* tonight, and your business is inside that.
- **Conditions the director shaped.** The concept, the staffing, the policies, the culture — you set these, and they colour what can happen.
- **Situations that no one authored.** A group arrives without booking. A critic is seated. A staff member is unwell. A supplier is late. The rain starts before service. A regular walks in with a stranger.
- **Responses that carry.** Whatever you did — hospitable, brisk, generous, cautious — is now part of what your business *is*, and will echo when the guest returns or when the story is told across the village.
- **A world that remembers.** Grythyttan does not reset. The guest you welcomed comes back. The mentor whose advice you took nods to you at a banquet. The supplier you paid on time offers you first pick.

### 9.2 Why the stories feel yours

Because you shaped the conditions that produced them, and only your specific conditions could have produced *these* stories. Two directors in two adjacent businesses will accumulate different regulars, different staff temperaments, different reputations, different memorable nights. The stories are not authored by the game; they are *emitted* by the game running around choices only you made.

### 9.3 The director as a storyteller

You will retell Nexus. Between shifts, to peer players, in the log, to a mentor NPC, at a banquet, at a case-study publication. This is not incidental. This is a *core loop of the game*: playing → remembering → telling → hearing others tell → returning to play with sharper attention.

### 9.4 The system that makes stories is the system that produces knowledge

The same events that generate a story you will remember also generate the understanding you will invest. This is not two systems. It is one. Every good story in Nexus leaves an entry in the log. Every log entry is the seed of a story.

## 10. What the organisation becomes

The organisation the player leads is alive over time (Constitution §9). It has an **understanding** of what it does, an **execution** of the work, and a **judgement** for what to do when the situation is not covered by anything it has done before. These are Aristotle's Episteme, Techne and Phronesis, and they are named in the Constitution — but they are used here as gameplay, never as philosophy class.

**Understanding (Episteme).** When strong, staff can explain the philosophy of the room in words a guest would recognise; the menu descriptions cohere; a complaint is diagnosed correctly; new hires ramp fast because the concept can be *told* to them. When weak, staff drift into inconsistency, the menu wanders, and new hires flounder because there is no story to teach.

**Execution (Techne).** When strong, service holds under pressure, the kitchen does not fumble, a run of covers arriving at once is absorbed without ripple. When weak, small mistakes accumulate and a dropped plate reads as a symptom of general looseness.

**Judgement (Phronesis).** When strong, a walk-in of nine is handled without disrupting seated tables, a guest's dietary crisis is met with grace, a supplier failure becomes a small pivot rather than a menu collapse. When weak, the novel produces disproportionate damage — staff freeze, defer, improvise badly. Judgement is what your director-level knowledge (Chapter 5) becomes when it lives in the organisation.

**How the director grows them.** By investing understandings into the organisation (§5.6). By culture, by hiring, by training, by codified policy, by exposure to varied situations, and by being a director whose own reading is deep enough to model.

**How the director reads them.** From the room. Not from a stat panel. A director who is watching sees them.

**How they interact.** Strong execution without judgement executes correctly in familiar situations and fails in unfamiliar ones. Strong understanding without execution knows what should happen and cannot make it happen. Strong judgement without either makes wise choices it cannot enact. The three are always *co-present* in what the room can do.

**How the interface treats them.** As invisible. No bars, no tiers, no achievements, no unlock chimes. The organisation's capacity is what the room *does*, not what a panel *says*.

## 11. Grythyttan matters — the Digital Twin as gameplay

Nexus is set in *real* Grythyttan (Directive §2). This is not lore. It is a gameplay foundation, and it makes possible the specific pleasure the game exists to offer.

### 11.1 A real place is a stronger medium than a procedural one

A procedural village produces every possible strategy. A real village produces the *right* strategies. The distance from a fisherman in Karlskoga to a kitchen in Grythyttan is a real distance and shapes what fish appears on which menu. The rhythm of Campus Grythyttan is a real rhythm and shapes when the village is thirsty. The Bergslagen weather is a real weather and shapes whether the terrace is set tonight. These are not constraints to route around. These are the medium a viable business is shaped by.

### 11.2 A real place makes verisimilitude a competitive advantage

Two directors' businesses can both be excellent. The one that fits Grythyttan will be excellent *because* it fits. That fit is not decoration; it is a legible quality of the business that other directors, and the game's guests, can feel.

### 11.3 A real place produces stories only a real place can

The story of a specific supplier in a specific neighbouring town, a specific tradition at a specific season, a specific building's history that constrained a redevelopment — these stories are not authored, and they are not procedurally emitted. They *belong to Grythyttan*, and Nexus is set there so that they can happen.

### 11.4 Gameplay emerges from Grythyttan, not the reverse

Every gameplay system in Nexus inherits the shape of the village it is played in. The strategy space, the market, the seasonal cycle, the community expectations, the buildings that are available, the buildings that are not — all follow from Grythyttan being what Grythyttan is. This framework is authored *above* the World Specification; it inherits its truthfulness.

Systems that would require ignoring or overwriting Grythyttan are not authorised.

## 12. Why the player returns

The 100-hour question. Answered honestly.

- **The world keeps making new situations.** No content pipeline. The village lives; the market moves; seasons turn; staff mature; suppliers change; guests age; competitors open and close. The situation queue is inexhaustible because it is not authored.
- **You see more each hour.** By hour 100 you notice a bottle held two seconds too long. You could not do this at hour 1. This deepening reading is the primary long-arc reward, and it is not measurable — it is felt.
- **Your organisation becomes a character.** The business grows its own tempo, its own staff, its own signature moves. Watching it at hour 80 is different in kind from watching a generic business at hour 3.
- **Grythyttan remembers you.** Guests return because they were welcomed. Suppliers offer terms because a debt was paid. A neighbour helps because they were helped. This memory is the fabric that makes long play feel *earned*.
- **Other players change what happens.** In multiplayer, the shared village produces situations no solo player could author. Peer directors compete, cooperate, mentor. Each new peer changes the situation pipeline for every other.
- **The market moves.** Strategies that worked last season stop working. Optimising is not stable. You keep re-learning the same village, which is not the same village any more.
- **Reputation compounds.** Late-game the director is offered opportunities early-game directors are not — banquet invitations, alumni roles, investor conversations, municipal projects. These are not level rewards; they are the consequence of who the director has become in the village.
- **The knowledge spiral has no top.** Every hour writes more into the log. Every entry sees the next hour a little better. The director is never done.
- **Reading a place is itself a craft.** This is the honest final answer. Nexus is enjoyable at hour 100 for the same reason attentive proprietorship is a real vocation people spend real lifetimes on: the reading is a craft, and Nexus is designed so the craft rewards attention at every hour of exposure.

### 12.1 What is deliberately not the reason

Not chimes. Not unlocks. Not completion percentages. Not grind. Not timers about to expire. Not a scoreboard. If any of these ever become part of the answer to Chapter 12, the game has failed this chapter and must be corrected.

## 13. What Nexus is not

Any of the following, if proposed, is refused under this framework:

- A hospitality tycoon with XP, levels, tokens or unlocks.
- A puppeteering RTS on the service floor.
- A first-person hero game in strategic play.
- A quest-giver adventure with objective markers.
- A dashboard game with a spreadsheet as primary interface.
- A morality-bar game.
- A clicker.
- A completionist collect-em-up with a percentage complete.
- A leaderboard game that ranks directors numerically.
- A cash shop for competence, ingredients, staff, reputation, or knowledge.
- A fictional-Grythyttan game that borrows the name.
- An interactive textbook about gastronomy.
- A university course wearing a game's skin.

Any proposal that reads as one of the above must be refused, and this framework cited.

## 14. The first playable prototype

A prototype that can be built inside one sprint, that is small, that is fun, that is representative, and that convinces a first-time developer that Nexus is a game.

### 14.1 Scope

**One shift, one business, one scenario, one condition to adjust, one visible consequence, one entry earned in the director's log.** Three to five minutes of real time.

### 14.2 What the sprint delivers

- **Continuous camera** across the three scales for a single gray-box district containing the player's business (Camera Bible §3).
- **Interior legibility.** Roof crossfades transparent below the interior threshold; interior activity legible from Kvarteret scale.
- **A living service.** Six to twelve NPCs — a small staff and a small guest population — moving through a shift with visible role, intention, destination. Each acts autonomously under the current conditions.
- **One condition surface.** A single policy the director can adjust — for example seating pace with three settings (brisk / considered / generous). The setting visibly changes staff behaviour and guest flow within a minute of sim time.
- **One scenario.** A group-arrival scenario (or its constitutional successor) fires once during the shift. Three concrete responses. The chosen response unfolds in the room over the following sim minutes. No popup resolves it.
- **One sustainability reading.** After the scenario resolves, one condition updates with direction + cause + expected consequence, in prose.
- **One director's log entry.** A single understanding is written into the log, in a sentence a person could say aloud, with the events that produced it attached. The player can open the log and re-read it.
- **Seeded determinism.** The whole shift runs from a single seed. Same seed, same policy, same response → same run (Camera Bible §15).
- **Accessible controls.** Mouse/keyboard and touch fully supported. Reduced motion honoured. WebGL fallback preserved.

### 14.3 What the sprint deliberately excludes

No knowledge economy beyond the single log entry. No multiplayer. No hiring, no negotiation, no investment. No banquets, no ceremonies, no publication. No decay, no market shift, no season change. No competitor businesses. No polished art. No sound beyond the existing ambience engine. No campus, no Måltidens Hus, no other buildings' interiors.

### 14.4 Why this prototype is representative

It instantiates the seven-beat loop in miniature. Observation: you watch the shift. Decision: you adjust seating pace based on what you see. Action: the setting commits and the world visibly registers. Consequence: the scenario fires and unfolds in the room. Reflection: the sustainability reading updates with a cause you recognise. Learning: the log entry names what happened. New opportunities: a second run with a different policy or a different response produces a different shift and a different entry.

### 14.5 The single acceptance test

A first-time developer, having played the prototype twice, must be able to say:

> "I understand why this is a game. I see how it becomes a hundred hours."

If they cannot, the prototype has failed on this framework and requires revision. If they can, the sprint is done.

## 15. Acceptance tests for every future system

This framework is the parent of every future gameplay system in Nexus. A new system is acceptable only when it passes every one of these tests, and its own document cites this framework for each pass.

- **Loop test.** The system participates in the seven-beat loop of Chapter 4. Any beat it skips is justified in the system's own document.
- **Verb test.** The system is played through verbs from Chapter 6, or introduces a new verb accepted into Chapter 6 by a numbered successor to this framework.
- **Knowledge-as-capital test.** The system contributes to or consumes knowledge (Chapter 5). The system never introduces a parallel XP surface, a parallel level bar, a parallel unlock tree, or any other currency of progression that competes with knowledge.
- **Decision test.** The system produces at least one meaningful decision of the six types in Chapter 7, with a real tradeoff between imperfect options.
- **Uncertainty test.** Any random draw is seeded (Constitution §8). No mechanic removes uncertainty in a way Chapter 8 forbids.
- **Story test.** The system contributes to the story-generating character of the game per Chapter 9. If it cannot produce a moment a player might tell a peer about, it does not belong.
- **Director test.** The system respects Chapter 3. No move-orders, no avatar in strategic play, no hero interfaces.
- **Organisation test.** Where the system involves capability, it moves understanding, execution or judgement (Chapter 10) *legibly in the world*, without showing them as numbers.
- **Twin test.** The system inherits from Chapter 11. It does not fictionalise Grythyttan and does not depend on unverified geography.
- **Return test.** The system contributes to at least one of the reasons in Chapter 12. It does not contribute to any of the deliberately-not reasons in Chapter 12.1.
- **Non-goal test.** The system does not read as any of the refused genres in Chapter 13.
- **Sprint test.** The system can be prototyped to a playable state within a defined sprint scope, per the pattern of Chapter 14.

A system that fails any of these tests is not authorised.

## 16. Open questions for the Vision Owner

- **GQ-01.** The director's log (§5.4): always visible, opened by a gesture, or surfaced only when a new entry writes? Interaction design work needed.
- **GQ-02.** Composition of understandings when two apply to the same situation: does the game *signal* the composition, or leave the director to notice?
- **GQ-03.** In multiplayer, are received understandings marked in the log as second-hand until confirmed, or merged silently?
- **GQ-04.** Does Nexus admit any avatar-mode content at all (per the reclassified Måltidens Hus vision document), and if so, under what conditions?
- **GQ-05.** The sustainability conditions (Camera Bible §8.2) and the five capitals (Constitution §6) still need a reconciled surface (Constitution C-01, M-03). Required before Priority 6.
- **GQ-06.** The prototype's first policy is proposed as seating pace (§14.2). Is this the correct first policy, or should it be one that more directly stresses the sustainability/capitals interaction?
- **GQ-07.** The prototype's first scenario is proposed as group-arrival (§14.2), matching Constitution §11 Priority 5. Confirm.
- **GQ-08.** Publication of understandings (§5.8): first-generation Knowledge Engine feature, or held for later?
- **GQ-09.** Loss of knowledge through staff turnover (§5.7): requires the human capital model to exist first (Priorities 4 and 6). Confirm this is a Priority 6+ feature.
- **GQ-10.** Do peer directors' choices in a shared village affect each other in the first prototype (§14), or is multiplayer explicitly out of sprint scope? This framework assumes out of scope for §14.
