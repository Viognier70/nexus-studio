## 1. Why this order exists

Nexus has no menu. Guests eat abstractly, `ingredientTier` is a three-position dial, and the ecological sustainability is a number nobody can point at.

The Vision Owner proposed the missing limb: **suppliers, a menu the player composes and prices, stock bought to last a service, and a live reading of how many plates remain of each dish.**

The reason it matters is one mechanic that cannot be reasoned around:

> Price a popular dish too low and it runs out before service ends — unless you planned for it.

That is a pricing lesson no formula teaches. It requires estimating demand, weighing margin against volume, and accepting that you cannot know until the room decides. **Plan, commit, watch it play out.** The same shape as everything else in the game, applied to the thing a restaurant actually sells.

It also makes the ecological reading concrete for the first time. Local lamb from Bergslagen versus a wholesaler is not an abstract point — it is two prices, two qualities and two delivery risks.

---

## 2. Suppliers

The player buys from named sources with real trade-offs. Not a tier dial: a set of relationships.

- **The wholesaler** — everything, always available, unremarkable, cheapest per unit.
- **Local growers** — vegetables and herbs from the district, seasonal, better quality, limited quantity, sometimes short.
- **Organic producers** in the surrounding area — the ecological reading's honest source.
- **Meat and game** — lamb, pork, game, from named local farms.
- **Lake fish** — genuinely local, genuinely limited, genuinely seasonal.
- **The local brewery**, and an outside drinks distributor.
- **Wine** — an importer, plus whatever the cellar already holds.
- **Equipment and furnishing** — furniture, tableware, kitchen equipment, wine fridges, at several price levels.

**Every supplier carries the same four axes**: price, quality, reliability, and what it does to the ecological reading. Report the set and its numbers before building. No supplier is strictly best — a wholesaler that never fails has its own argument.

Reliability matters: a supplier that occasionally short-delivers is cheaper, and the risk is the player's to price in. `ORDER 045`'s roadworks factor already slows deliveries; this is the same texture with the player's choice behind it.

---

## 3. The menu

After the loan, the premises, the renovation and the kitchen upgrade, the player **composes the menu and the wine list, and sets the prices.**

- Dishes are built from ingredients the player has bought.
- The wine list is priced from what the cellar holds.
- Prices are the player's to set, bounded by `ORDER 050` Addendum A §11 — **the house cannot ask more than it has earned the right to ask.**

**Package menus** — three, five or seven courses at a set price with a composed drinks pairing — are a distinct decision: a guaranteed spend per cover against a fixed cost and a committed stock draw. Report how they interact with à la carte before building.

---

## 4. Stock, and running out

**A live reading of the menu shows every dish and how many plates remain.**

This is where pricing shows its teeth. A dish priced attractively sells fast; if the player did not buy stock to match, it runs out — and the room reports it. A guest orders something the kitchen cannot serve, the waiter has to say so, and the consequence lands where consequences land: satisfaction, reputation, and the stream saying what happened.

The wine list works the same way. A well-priced bottle empties.

Requirements:

- **Stock is bought before service** and drawn down as guests order.
- **The plates-remaining reading is visible during service** — the player watches the number fall.
- **Running out is a real event**, not a silent substitution. It posts to the stream and it costs something.
- **Leftover stock persists** but is not free: ingredients age, and `ORDER 043`'s waste already exists as a concept to build on.

---

## 5. How this connects to what exists

- **Every purchase posts a ledger line** (`ORDER 050` §3). Buying stock is where the money goes, and the business account shows it.
- **`ingredientTier` is superseded.** It was a stand-in for exactly this; retire it and reroute what reads it.
- **The ecological reading** draws from actual sourcing, not a policy setting.
- **`ORDER 050`'s activities remain** — the supplier relationship (a seasonal contract, a certification) is an activity; the weekly buying is not.
- **Kitchen competence matters**: what the team can execute limits what the menu can carry. Report how.

---

## 6. Scope discipline

Two risks, named so they can be watched:

**This is a large amount of data** — dishes, ingredients, suppliers, wines. Start with a set small enough to balance and large enough to force a real choice. Report the size before authoring it.

**Menu composition can become a game of its own** competing with the service for attention. The menu is set in the morning and stands; it is not adjusted mid-service. Per `ORDER 050` Addendum A §6.3, one thing per surface.

## 7. Build order

1. **Suppliers** — the set, the four axes, the numbers. Report first.
2. **Ingredients and stock** — buying, holding, drawing down.
3. **The menu** — composition, pricing, the plates-remaining reading.
4. **Running out** — the event, its cost, its appearance in the stream.
5. **The wine list**, same shape.
6. **Package menus** — after à la carte holds.
7. **Retire `ingredientTier`** and reroute its readers.

Report after 1 and 3.

## 8. Acceptance

The Vision Owner plays and reports:

1. That choosing a supplier was a real trade-off, not a quality dial with names on it.
2. That setting a price felt like a bet on how the room would respond.
3. That watching plates run down during service was worth watching.
4. That running out of a dish was a consequence he could trace to his own pricing.
5. That the ecological reading finally pointed at something he had actually done.
6. That the morning's planning and the evening's outcome felt like the same decision, seen twice.

---

## 9. What this order does not do

No multiplayer, no persistence, no new geometry. The plates-remaining reading is a panel, not plates rendered in the room — though a dish selling out could eventually change what the guests at a table are doing, and that is worth a later order.

---

## 10. What this order is for

A restaurant sells food at a price. Until now Nexus has had neither.

This order gives the player the thing every restaurateur actually decides — what to serve, what to charge, and how much to buy — and lets the room answer.
